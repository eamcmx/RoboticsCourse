Joint limits and singularities

Where the math breaks down. Some configurations are unreachable because the joints can't physically rotate that far; others are reachable but mathematically degenerate. Both matter for practical IK.

Joint limits — physical bounds

Every joint has a physical end-stop. The math is happy to compute solutions outside those limits, but the robot will refuse to move there. So the IK solver has to know about the limits and clamp solutions inside them. The Panda has asymmetric limits — joint 4 can only travel from −175° to −4°. Forget that and your "solution" is unreachable.

Every revolute joint has minimum and maximum angles set by the physical end-stops. The Panda — for example — has joint 4 limited to (−175°, −4°): only one side of zero. The IK solver must respect these limits, either by clamping each q after the Newton update, or by adding a barrier term to the residual.

Singularities — algebraic degeneracies

A configuration q* is singular when the Jacobian J(q*) loses rank. Geometrically, this means there's some direction of task-space motion that no joint motion can produce. Two common types:

  • Boundary singularities — the arm is at the edge of its workspace, fully extended or fully folded. Some directions of motion would require the arm to extend past its reach.
  • Internal singularities — wrist alignment, elbow alignment. The arm can be in the workspace and still have a degenerate Jacobian.

Detecting singularities in code

The cheap test is to compute det(J) for square Jacobians, or the smallest singular value σmin(J) for rectangular ones. Near zero means near-singular. The "manipulability" measure

is a popular scalar summary. It's zero exactly at singularities and grows away from them.

Handling them in IK

Near a singularity the Newton step explodes — you ask for a small task-space motion and get a huge joint velocity. The standard fix is the damped pseudo-inverse: instead of inv(J) you use (JᵀJ + λ²I)⁻¹Jᵀ. The damping λ pulls the step back to something sane at the cost of a small steady-state error.

Practical mitigation:

  1. Damped least squares. Replace J+ with (JᵀJ + λ²I)⁻¹Jᵀ. The damping λ trades exact-tracking-when-far-from-singular against not-blowing-up-when-close.
  2. Manipulability gradient. Add a secondary objective that pushes q away from singular regions — useful for redundant arms (n > 6).
  3. Path planning around them. If the trajectory passes through a singularity, plan a way around it in joint space.

Quick recap

  • Joint limits are physical bounds; clamp or barrier them into your IK.
  • Singularities are configurations where J loses rank — some task-space motions become impossible.
  • Detect via det(J), σmin(J), or manipulability w(q).
  • Handle via damped pseudo-inverse (DLS) and singularity-aware path planning.
