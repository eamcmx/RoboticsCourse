Lecture 05 · Module 2

Rigid-body motion — a reusable box function

Apply T to an actual rigid body, not just a frame. Watch a simple cube translate, rotate, and chain through composite transforms — the same primitive we'll lift into every robot link in the rest of the module.

Why we need to move objects, not just frames

So far T has been an abstract machine that moves frames around. The point of all this is to move things — links, tools, payloads. Every link in a robot is a rigid body, and the link's pose is just T applied to its body coordinates.

Up to here we've described the pose of one frame relative to another. The reason we cared about the frame is that everything attached to it moves with it. A link of a robot is a rigid body — a set of points whose mutual distances never change. To draw the link, we describe a few of its corner points in the link's own frame, and then transform those points into the world using T.

That's the whole pattern: define a body once in its local coordinates, define T to place that body in the world, multiply. Repeat for every link of every robot, every joint of every chain. This lecture builds the smallest version of that pattern — a single cube — so the move from cube → link → robot in the next lecture has nothing new in it.

Defining a rigid body in its own frame

A unit cube has eight vertices. In the cube's body frame they sit at the eight combinations of (±0.5, ±0.5, ±0.5). Twelve edges connect them. To draw the cube anywhere in the world, all we have to do is multiply each of those eight body-frame points by the same T, then redraw the edges between the transformed corners.

The widget below has a unit cube in its body frame. The six sliders drive a homogeneous transform T (three rotations, three translations). The cube is rendered using T applied to its eight vertices.

The tracked point is one corner of the cube

The blue dot in the widget is the corner of the cube at (0.5, 0.5, 0.5) in body coordinates. As you move the sliders, watch the corner's fixed-frame coordinates update in the readout. Every other corner of the cube moves the same way — body-frame coordinates fixed forever, world-frame coordinates determined by T.

Translate-then-rotate, again — but on a real body

Module 1 closed with the observation that translate-then-rotate doesn't equal rotate-then-translate. The same statement now has a concrete meaning: applying the two compositions to a cube places the cube at two visibly different poses, even though both T matrices were built from the same six numbers.

Use the sandbox to compute both compositions and inspect the resulting matrices side by side. The difference is entirely in the right column — the translation block — but it's enough to put the cube in two different parts of the workspace.

The two right columns differ; the rotation blocks are identical. The take-away — write down the order of operations explicitly when you compose, and pick the convention that matches the physical interpretation you want. For robot kinematics we'll always want "rotate the link first, then push it along the rotated axis" — which is the right side of the multiplication, i.e., post-multiplication.

Where this is heading

The next lecture introduces the simplest possible robot — three prismatic joints — and you'll see that the way it moves is exactly this same operation, applied three times in series. Each joint is a small T; the end-effector is the product of all the small Ts; and you already know how to read a 4×4 transform off a slider. We're not learning anything new in Lecture 06. We're just using what we have on a longer chain.

Quick recap

  • Rigid-body geometry is defined once in the body's own frame; world geometry is recomputed by multiplying body coordinates by T.
  • For a cube, that means transforming 8 corners and redrawing 12 edges. The principle scales identically to a robot link.
  • Composition order matters at the rigid-body level just as it did at the frame level — same six numbers, different cube poses.
  • Forward kinematics is the natural extension: T_n = T_1 · T_2 · … · T_n, with each T_i contributing one joint variable.
Homework 05 · due before next session

Build your own box function

  1. By hand: write the eight vertex coordinates of a unit cube centred at the origin in its body frame. Pick T = Rz(45°) · Ttr(0.5, 0, 0). Compute the world-frame coordinates of each vertex.
  2. In MATLAB or the browser sandbox: write a function draw_box(T) that takes a 4×4 transform and plots the cube at that pose. Use it to render three different poses on the same axes.
  3. Animate the cube along a sweep: 360° rotation around z while translating from (0, 0, 0) to (1, 0, 0). Use a for loop with 60 frames.
  4. Modify the function to take a body-frame definition as input — i.e., pass the eight vertices in. Draw two different rigid bodies (a wide rectangle, a thin one) using the same function.
