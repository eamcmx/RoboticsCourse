Rotation around Y and Z, composite rotations

The same projection logic, applied to the other two axes — and what happens when we stack them. Composition is right-to-left, and order matters.

Quick recap of last time

Okay, so let's continue with our class. Just for a quick recap, let's try to remember what we have seen on the last lessons.

Last time we built the rotation matrix Rx(θ) by projecting the mobile-frame axes (u, v, w) onto the fixed axes (x, y, z). The columns of the matrix are exactly those projections — that is the whole trick. When θ = 0 we got the identity I₃; for any other θ we got the familiar

Today we're doing two things. First, exactly the same trick for rotations around y and around z — those should be quick. Then, the more interesting question: what happens when we apply more than one rotation?

Rotation around Y

The same logic. Believe me, there are no surprises. v stays put because it's along y; u and w move.

Now the rotation is around the fixed y-axis. v doesn't move — it's along y. u (originally along x) tilts toward z; w (originally along z) tilts back toward −x. Drag the slider to see it, then build the matrix.

Notice the sign pattern

The minus sign sits on a different entry than in Rx. For Ry the −sin θ is at row 1, column 3. That isn't an arbitrary convention — it follows from the right-hand rule on the projection of w onto x. When we get to inverse kinematics later in the term, that sign will matter.

Rotation around Z

And around z. w stays put — it's along z. u and v rotate together in the x-y plane. This is the rotation you've intuitively done a thousand times when you turn a piece of paper flat on a desk.

Composite rotations

Okay so what happens if we want to do more than one rotation? Let's say first we rotate around x, then around y, then around z. How do we represent that?

In robotics we usually multiply rotations right-to-left. The leftmost matrix is the last rotation that gets applied; the rightmost is the first. Why? Order matters

Just remember that in matrix algebra the multiplication of matrices is not commutative. So that means that A times B is not the same as B times A.

Matrix multiplication is not commutative. Rx · Ry · Rz is generally not equal to Rz · Ry · Rx. Physically, that means rotating 90° around y and then 90° around x lands you in a different place than rotating 90° around x and then 90° around y. The widget above and the sandbox below let you verify that with the same numbers.

Convention warning

Different textbooks and software packages use different multiplication orders and different sign conventions. The course's convention is right-to-left composition with the right-hand rule for sign. When you read a paper or a manual, the first thing to check is which convention the author is using. We'll come back to this when we hit the Denavit–Hartenberg standard in Lecture 10.

Quick recap

  • Ry(θ) and Rz(θ) follow the same projection logic as Rx(θ); only the sign pattern shifts.
  • To compose rotations, multiply the matrices right-to-left.
  • Order matters — matrix multiplication is not commutative.
  • At θ = 0 every elementary rotation collapses to I₃, so a composite of zero rotations is also I₃ — good sanity check.
