Lecture 01 · Module 1 · Robot Dynamics

From kinematics to dynamics

Why forces and torques — not just geometry — decide whether a robot actually works.

Lecturer. bibliosense Lecture. 01 Course. Robot Dynamics
Kinematics told us where the robot is and how its parts move relative to each other. That's the map. Today we step into the physics that makes the journey possible — the forces and torques behind every motion. We'll see why a heavy payload changes everything, why an extended arm sags under its own weight, and why the equation \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\) is the single most important line in this whole course.

0What this lecture covers

This module bridges what you already know — robot kinematics — with the new world of robot dynamics. We'll establish why the transition matters, classify the two big problem types (forward and inverse dynamics), introduce the fundamental equation of motion, and look at where dynamic modelling makes or breaks real engineering applications.

By the end you should be comfortable with the equation \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\), know what each term physically represents, and understand when you'd use forward vs. inverse dynamics in practice.

Think of this lecture as the why before the how. The maths comes in Module 2 — today is about building the right mental model.
Learning objectives. Conceptual foundation, problem classification, the role of forces and torques, and the mathematical preparation that guides the rest of the course.

1From kinematics to dynamics: the essential transition

Kinematics gives you the geometric backbone — forward kinematics for end-effector position, inverse kinematics for joint angles, Jacobians for velocity, differentiation for acceleration. You can predict where the robot will be at any time. That's powerful, but it's incomplete.

Kinematics cannot answer the questions that actually matter on the factory floor: How much torque does this actuator need? Why does the robot overshoot during rapid moves? How does a heavy payload change the motor requirements? For those, we need dynamics.

Robot dynamics asks the next question: what forces and torques cause the motion? That single shift — from describing motion to explaining its physical causes — is what separates a paper trajectory from a working robot.

Kinematics is the map; dynamics is the fuel, the engine, and the terrain. You need both.
Hierarchical structure. Dynamics doesn't replace kinematics — it contains it. Every dynamic equation has positions, velocities, and accelerations baked into it as kinematic ingredients.
Configuration dependency. The effective inertia 'seen' by a motor changes with robot posture. A motor that easily moves an arm in one pose may stall in another, even with the same payload.

2Why dynamic modelling is non-negotiable

Dynamic modelling is the cornerstone of practical robotics, not an academic luxury. The gap between a robot that works in a lab and one that performs reliably in industry usually comes down to model quality.

Without dynamics you cannot design effective controllers (you don't know what forces to apply), you cannot select actuators (you don't know what torques are needed), and you cannot optimise performance (you don't understand the energy flows). Compare moving a pencil on paper to moving a 100 kg automotive part at high speed with millimetre precision — the geometric path is similar, but the dynamic requirements are worlds apart.

The complexity comes from four physical effects that all act simultaneously:

  • Inertial coupling — accelerating one joint accelerates every link attached to it.
  • Gravitational loading — each link's weight produces a posture-dependent torque.
  • Velocity-dependent forces — Coriolis and centrifugal effects emerge when joints move together.
  • Configuration dependency — the effective inertia at each actuator changes with posture.

This is why naïve approaches (just crank up the motor torques, slap on a basic feedback loop) fail spectacularly as speed and precision demands rise.

Newton's \(\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}\) still rules — but on a multi-link arm it becomes a tangle of coupled, nonlinear differential equations.
The physics behind the necessity. Inertial coupling, gravity, and velocity-dependent forces all change continuously as the robot moves. Static intuition will mislead you in dynamic regimes.
Where it bites. Precision manufacturing (surface finish, dimensional accuracy) and surgical robotics (tremor-free motion, force limiting, patient safety) are two domains where bad dynamic models cause real harm.

The four effects we just listed — inertial coupling, gravitational loading, velocity-dependent (Coriolis/centrifugal) forces, and configuration dependency — never act alone, but each one dominates a particular failure mode. You should come away able to map a physical observation onto a specific slot in the equation of motion.

3Case study: automotive welding robot

A 6-DOF arm welds car body panels at speeds up to 1.5 m/s with positional accuracy of ±0.1 mm. This is the textbook case for why dynamic modelling is essential, not optional.

Inertial effects during high-speed motion. Rapid acceleration develops massive inertial forces. Position feedback alone cannot react fast enough to correct these predictable disturbances. The fix is feedforward control using inverse dynamics — the controller computes \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\) to predict and cancel inertial forces before they cause tracking error.

Gravitational compensation. An extended horizontal arm sags under its own weight, often by several millimetres — well beyond the ±0.1 mm tolerance. Real-time computation of \(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\) continuously cancels gravity, making the arm appear weightless to the position controller.

Velocity-dependent effects. Coriolis and centrifugal forces during curved motion can demand 20–30% of total actuator capacity. Compensating for \(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\) in real time keeps weld quality consistent regardless of path.

Quantitative impact. Accuracy improved from ±0.5 mm to ±0.05 mm (10× better), production speed up 40%, energy consumption down 25%, scrap rate down 85%. The dynamic-modelling investment paid back in six months.

This is the punchline: better physics in the model means tighter tolerances, faster cycles, and lower energy bills — all at once.
Why feedforward wins. Feedback alone cannot anticipate inertial or gravitational forces — it only reacts. Feedforward from inverse dynamics cancels them before they become tracking errors.

4Forward vs. inverse dynamics

Robot dynamics naturally splits into two complementary problem classes. Both use the same physics — they just point in opposite directions.

Forward dynamics answers prediction: given the torques I apply, what motion results? It's the simulation problem — solve for \(\ddot{\mathbf{q}}\) given \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\).

Inverse dynamics answers planning: given the motion I want, what torques do I need? It's the control problem — solve for \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) given \(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}, \ddot{\mathbf{q}}\).

A 7-DOF surgical robot uses both: inverse dynamics for pre-operative trajectory planning and feedforward control, forward dynamics for real-time safety monitoring — predicting motion under current inputs and flagging discrepancies that might indicate unexpected tissue contact.

Same equation, opposite direction. Forward = 'what will happen?' Inverse = 'what should I command?'
They work together. In real systems the two are complementary: inverse dynamics drives the feedforward command, forward dynamics validates the result and watches for anomalies.
Forward dynamics — solve for accelerations
q̈ = D⁻¹(q) [ τ − h(q, q̇) − c(q) ]
Inverse dynamics — solve for torques
τ = D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q)

The two directions of dynamics solve the same equation but with opposite knowns and unknowns: forward dynamics solves \(\ddot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{\tau} - \mathbf{h} - \mathbf{c}]\) for motion given torque, while inverse dynamics solves \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c}\) for torque given a planned trajectory. 5The fundamental equation of robot motion

Every dynamic analysis in this course rests on one equation:

\[\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\]

Where:

  • \(\boldsymbol{\tau}\) — \(n \times 1\) vector of joint torques (or forces, for prismatic joints)
  • \(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\) — \(n \times n\) inertia matrix, symmetric and positive definite
  • \(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\) — \(n \times 1\) vector of Coriolis and centrifugal terms
  • \(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\) — \(n \times 1\) vector of gravitational forces

Learn to read this equation physically. The \(\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}\) term is 'mass times acceleration' for the whole arm — but the mass is a matrix because joints are coupled. The \(\mathbf{h}\) term is the price you pay for moving — Coriolis from joint–joint interaction, centrifugal from a joint's own motion. The \(\mathbf{c}\) term is just gravity, and depends only on configuration.

If you remember one thing from this lecture, remember this equation. We'll spend the rest of the course unpacking, deriving, and using it.
Read it physically. \(\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}\) = inertia × acceleration. \(\mathbf{h}\) = velocity-dependent forces. \(\mathbf{c}\) = gravity. Three terms, three sources of torque demand.

This section names the three sources of joint torque demand: \(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}\) (inertia × acceleration, with off-diagonal coupling), \(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\) (Coriolis from joint–joint interaction plus centrifugal from a joint's own velocity), and \(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\) (gravity, posture-only). You should leave able to predict, before doing any maths, which of the three terms vanish in any given operating regime.

6Properties of the dynamic matrices

The matrices in the equation of motion aren't arbitrary — they have structural properties that we exploit constantly in control design.

Inertia matrix \(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\). Symmetric and positive definite: \(\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{D}^T\) and \(\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{D} \mathbf{x} > 0\) for all \(\mathbf{x} \neq 0\). This guarantees energy conservation and underpins stability proofs. It changes with configuration; off-diagonal entries are inertial coupling between joints.

Coriolis/centrifugal \(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\). Quadratic in velocity: \(\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}, \alpha\dot{\mathbf{q}}) = \alpha^2 \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}})\). Often written as \(\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}\). The matrix \(\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}\) is skew-symmetric — a passivity property that's gold for nonlinear control design.

Gravity \(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\). Position-dependent only — independent of velocity. It's a conservative force, derivable from a potential energy function. Bounded for any finite configuration.

These properties aren't decoration — they're the toehold that lets us prove a controller actually works.
Skew-symmetry = passivity. \(\dot{\mathbf{D}} - 2\mathbf{C}\) being skew-symmetric is the property that makes passivity-based control of robots possible. Remember it.
Boundedness. \(\mathbf{D}\) is bounded above and below; \(\mathbf{h}\) is bounded by quadratics in velocity; \(\mathbf{c}\) is bounded for any finite pose. These bounds are the basis for robustness analysis.

7How dynamics plugs into a control system

Dynamic modelling and control design are symbiotic — better models enable better control. A typical robot control architecture looks like this:

  1. Trajectory planner generates the desired motion: \(\mathbf{q}_d(t), \dot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t), \ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d(t)\).
  2. Inverse dynamics (feedforward) computes \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} = \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}}) + \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{q})\).
  3. Feedback controller adds corrective torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}\) from tracking error.
  4. Total command: \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{total} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{ff} + \boldsymbol{\tau}_{fb}\).
  5. Plant (forward dynamics) — the actual robot — responds with real motion.

Feedforward does the heavy lifting; feedback only mops up the uncertainties (model errors, disturbances, payload changes). Without an accurate dynamic model, feedback has to do everything itself, which is why model-free controllers tend to be sluggish, overshoot, or chatter at high gains.

Feedforward predicts. Feedback corrects. Get the model right and feedback's job becomes easy.
The model is leverage. Every newton-metre that feedforward delivers correctly is a newton-metre that feedback doesn't have to chase. Better model → less feedback gain needed → better stability margin.
Computed-torque control law
τ_ff   = D(q) q̈_d + h(q, q̇) + c(q)     # feedforward (inverse dynamics)
τ_fb   = K_p e + K_d ė                    # feedback on tracking error
τ_cmd  = τ_ff + τ_fb                      # total joint command

This section frames computed-torque control as a five-stage loop: planner → feedforward (inverse dynamics) → feedback → summed command → plant (forward dynamics). The animator below steps through one control tick on a 2-DOF arm and lets you toggle the feedforward branch on or off, with a live readout of tracking error \(e = \mathbf{q}_d - \mathbf{q}\) and required feedback torque. Switch off feedforward and notice the same trajectory now demands much larger feedback torques — that is exactly the leverage the section calls out: every newton-metre delivered correctly by \(\mathbf{D}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}_d + \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{c}\) is one fewer that feedback has to chase.
