Lecture 22 · Module 5

Introduction to robot dynamics

Twenty-one lectures of kinematics gave us the geometry of motion. Dynamics asks the next question: what forces and torques cause this motion? The whole rest of this course — actuator sizing, motion control, energy budgets — depends on the answer.

Kinematics tells you where, dynamics tells you how

Kinematics is the map. It tells you the route from A to B and where every joint is along the way. Dynamics is the engine, the fuel, and the terrain. It tells you how much torque each motor needs, when the arm will overshoot, and whether the trajectory is even physically possible. You can plan a beautiful path with kinematics alone, but the moment you ask a real motor to track it, dynamics shows up.

You already know how to compute end-effector positions, solve inverse kinematics, build Jacobians, and animate trajectories. None of that has touched mass, inertia, gravity, or torque. That's the gap dynamics fills. The relationship is hierarchical: dynamics contains kinematics — the equations of motion still need positions, velocities, and accelerations as inputs — but it adds physical reality on top.

Three concrete questions kinematics cannot answer, and dynamics can:

  • Actuator sizing. What peak torque does joint 2 need to swing a 5 kg payload from rest to 1 m/s in 0.4 s?
  • Why does it overshoot? A robot with a perfectly tuned PD controller still oscillates when payload changes. Where does the wobble come from?
  • Energy. How much electrical power does the cell consume per pick-and-place cycle, and how do I cut it?

Newton's second law, but with coupling

Every dynamic system obeys F = ma. For a single rigid body, that's a one-line equation. For an n-link manipulator it's a coupled, configuration-dependent system. Three things make it complicated:

  1. Inertial coupling. Accelerating joint 1 doesn't just accelerate link 1 — it accelerates everything attached to it. The effective inertia at joint 1 changes with configuration: an extended arm presents more inertia than a folded one.
  2. Velocity-dependent forces. When two joints move simultaneously, Coriolis and centrifugal terms appear. They don't exist in static analysis but become substantial at high speed.
  3. Configuration-dependent gravity. Each link's gravitational torque depends on the orientation of every link before it.

Forward dynamics vs inverse dynamics

The same equation goes two ways. Read it left to right and you get inverse dynamics — given the trajectory you want, compute the torques needed to produce it. Read it right to left and you get forward dynamics — given the torques the motors are applying, predict what the robot will do. Both are useful, in different parts of the pipeline.

Inverse dynamics is the one your controller does in real time. You plan a smooth trajectory qd(t), q̇d(t), q̈d(t), and at each tick you compute the torques required to achieve it. That's exactly what the green arm in the widget above is doing 240 times a second.

Forward dynamics is the one your simulator does. Given the torques being applied right now, integrate to find the next state. That's what every entry in the torque plot above feeds into: an RK4 step that updates positions and velocities. It's also how you safely test a new controller before deploying it on the real robot — let the simulated forward dynamics catch any instabilities.

Same equation, two directions, two purposes. Keep them straight: when someone says "compute the dynamics," ask which direction.

Where this fits in a control pipeline

The structure that nearly every modern robot controller uses, sketched out:

  1. Trajectory planner outputs qd(t), q̇d(t), q̈d(t).
  2. Inverse-dynamics block (feedforward) computes τff = D q̈d + h(q, q̇) + c(q) using the desired trajectory and the measured state.
  3. Feedback block adds a small corrective torque τfb proportional to position and velocity errors.
  4. Robot sees τtotal = τff + τfb. The feedforward does the heavy lifting; the feedback handles uncertainties (modelling errors, friction, payload changes).
  5. Sensors measure q, q̇ and feed them back to step 2.

Without the feedforward block, feedback gains have to be cranked up enormously to compensate for inertia and gravity, which makes the system noisy, energy-hungry, and prone to instability. With it, feedback gains can be small and the loop stays well-behaved. That's the practical payoff of dynamic modelling.

Why this matters for your assessment

The final assignment of the Kinematics half (Lecture 20, master-slave) only needed position-level coordination. The dynamics half opens the door to performance-level questions: "is this trajectory achievable with these motors?", "how do I reduce cycle time without sacrificing precision?", "what is the energy bill per cycle?". These are exam-quality questions, and they're all impossible to answer without the equation we just met.

Quick recap

  • Kinematics describes motion geometrically; dynamics describes the forces and torques that cause it.
  • The equation of motion for any serial manipulator: τ = D(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) + c(q).
  • D is the configuration-dependent inertia matrix, h captures Coriolis and centrifugal effects, c captures gravity.
  • Inverse dynamics (used by controllers) takes a trajectory and outputs torques.
  • Forward dynamics (used by simulators) takes torques and outputs accelerations to integrate.
  • A computed-torque controller using inverse dynamics tracks reference trajectories far more cleanly than pure PD — especially under high speed or heavy payload.
