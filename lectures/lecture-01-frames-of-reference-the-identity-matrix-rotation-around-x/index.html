Lecture 01 · Module 1

Frames of reference, the identity matrix, rotation around X

Two coordinate frames, six degrees of freedom, and the first matrix you'll meet — derived live by projecting unit vectors onto axes.

Welcome

First lecture. There is a lot of new vocabulary in the next 90 minutes — frames of reference, projections, rotation matrices — and you don't have to retain everything on first contact. Every concept here will be used again, ten times, before the term is over.

This is the first lecture of Kinematics and Dynamics of Robot Manipulators. The whole semester is going to be one connected worked example — we'll start with a single point in space and build it all the way up to two industrial robots running a master–slave choreography on a moving plate. Today is the first piece: how do we describe where something is in 3D space, mathematically, in a way a computer can use?

We're going to be using MATLAB and RoboDK in the live course; on this page we'll use a browser-based sandbox that runs the same matrix algebra so you can experiment without leaving the lecture. The convention is the same in both: x is red, y is green, z is blue.

Degrees of freedom

From the prerequisites: a degree of freedom is one independent direction in which a rigid body can move. A free body in 3D space has six — three to translate, three to rotate. The whole job of kinematics is bookkeeping on which are free and which are constrained by the joints.

A free body in 3D space has six degrees of freedom — three for translation (along x, y, z) and three for rotation (around x, y, z). When we attach a robot to that body, the robot's joints constrain those degrees of freedom. Each joint either contributes a degree of freedom (a rotational or prismatic motion the robot can choose) or removes one. The whole job of kinematics is to keep track of which are free, which are constrained, and how they compose.

The fact that translations and rotations are independent is what lets us separate the matrix that describes position from the matrix that describes orientation, and that's the move we're going to make next.

Reference frames

Position is always relative. To say where something is we need a reference — three orthogonal unit vectors x, y, z meeting at an origin. Every coordinate, every transform, every robot pose in this course will live relative to a frame like this one.

To talk about where something is, we need something to talk relative to. In robotics that's almost always a Cartesian frame: three orthogonal unit vectors x, y, z meeting at an origin. In the lecture I'd draw it on the whiteboard; here you can drag the camera around it.

When we have two frames — say, one fixed at the base of the robot (call it frame 0) and one attached to the tool (call it frame 1) — we need a way to describe how frame 1 sits relative to frame 0. That description is going to be a matrix. The matrix lives between the two frames.

The identity matrix — frames that live together happily

Two frames perfectly overlapping is the trivial case. Every column of the rotation matrix encodes one mobile axis projected onto the fixed axes — and at perfect alignment, those projections are exactly the standard basis. The result is the identity matrix.

The simplest possible case: the two frames coincide. Same origin, same orientation, every axis pointing the same way. Their relationship is "nothing has changed". Let's express that mathematically.

We use unit vectors. If u is the x-axis of the mobile frame, how much of u lies along x of the fixed frame? All of it — the projection is 1. How much of u lies along y? Zero. Along z? Zero. The columns of the rotation matrix are exactly the components of u, v, w expressed in the fixed frame's x, y, z.

We get the identity matrix, written I₃ — the 3×3 matrix with ones on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else. Whenever you see I₃ in a derivation, read it as "no rotation has happened yet".

Rotation around X

Now the mobile frame is rotated by θ around the fixed x axis. u stays put because it lies along x; v and w both swing through θ in the y–z plane. Project each one onto x, y, z and read the resulting columns — that is the matrix.

Now the mobile frame rotates by an angle θ around the fixed x axis. u doesn't move — it stays along x. But v tilts up out of the y direction, and w tilts back along y. We derive the matrix the same way: project each mobile axis onto each fixed axis.

At θ = 0, every cos becomes 1 and every sin becomes 0 — the matrix collapses to I₃, which is exactly what should happen when the two frames coincide. At θ = 90°, cos = 0 and sin = 1, so v ends up fully along z and w ends up fully along −y. Drag the widget slider to 90° and check that the picture matches.

Animating a point

Pick a point a fixed inside the mobile frame at coordinates (0.3, 0.4, 0.5). As the frame rotates, the point moves with it — but its description in the mobile frame's own coordinates never changes. The whole job of R is to translate that fixed mobile description into the fixed-frame coordinates we can actually plot.

Take a point a = (0.3, 0.4, 0.5) defined in the mobile frame. As the mobile frame rotates around x, the point's coordinates relative to the fixed frame change — even though, from the mobile frame's point of view, the point hasn't moved at all. Drag the slider to watch the point sweep out an arc.

That's the whole idea of representing motion as matrix multiplication. We'll do this thousands of times this semester — with bigger matrices, with more frames, with translations folded in — but the idea is the same. The matrix lives between two frames; multiplying takes you across.

Quick recap

  • A free body has six degrees of freedom — three translations, three rotations.
  • To describe orientation, attach a reference frame to it. We use Cartesian frames with x-red, y-green, z-blue.
  • The relationship between two frames is captured by a 3×3 rotation matrix.
  • The columns of the matrix are the mobile frame's axes (u, v, w) expressed in the fixed frame's basis (x, y, z).
  • When the frames coincide, the matrix is the identity I₃.
  • For a rotation by θ around x, u stays put; v and w rotate in the y–z plane. Hence Rx(θ).
  • To find a point's coordinates in the fixed frame, multiply: a_xyz = R · a_uvw.
