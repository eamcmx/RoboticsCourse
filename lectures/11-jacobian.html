The Jacobian — joint space ↔ task space

A single matrix that says “if you nudge each joint by a little, the end-effector moves like this.” The bridge between the world of qs and the world of xs — and the object every velocity controller, every iterative IK solver, and every singularity diagnostic is built on top of.

The bridge between two spaces

Forward kinematics gives us a non-linear map from joint space to task space. The Jacobian is its derivative — the local linear approximation. With it we can answer two questions we couldn't before: what end-effector velocity comes out for a given joint velocity, and which joint velocities will produce a desired end-effector velocity.

Up to now, every map we've built has been a non-linear function. The end-effector pose depends on the joint values through a long chain of trig identities. To do anything iterative — track a path, invert the kinematics, control velocity — we need a linear approximation we can manipulate. That linearisation is the Jacobian J(q).

For a robot with n joints whose end-effector pose is described by an m-dimensional output (positions, possibly orientations), J is the m×n matrix of partial derivatives:

That equation is the whole lecture in one line. The end-effector velocity is a linear combination of the columns of J, with the joint rates as coefficients. Move joint 1 alone, and the end-effector moves along column 1 of J. Move joint 2 alone, along column 2. Move them together, and you superimpose.

Walkthrough: the 2-link planar arm

We'll do the derivation by hand on the smallest case that's still interesting — a planar arm with two revolute joints and link lengths L₁ and L₂. The forward kinematics is short enough to fit on one line:

That tells us straight away when the Jacobian goes singular: when sin(q₂) = 0 — that is, when the elbow is fully extended (q₂ = 0) or fully folded (q₂ = ±π). At those poses, the two columns of J become parallel; the matrix loses rank; and certain end-effector motions become unreachable no matter how fast we spin the joints. See each column do its job

The widget below is the Jacobian made visible. The two q sliders place the arm. The two q-dot sliders set how fast each joint is moving. The red arrow at the tip is what joint 1 alone does to the tool. The green arrow is what joint 2 alone does. The blue arrow is the sum — the actual tool velocity for the joint rates you picked.

Try this experiment: set q̇₂ = 0 (only joint 1 is moving) and watch how the red arrow at the tip swings as you change q₁ and q₂. That arrow is column 1 of J scaled by q̇₁. It is always perpendicular to the line from the base to the tip — because joint 1 rotates the whole arm rigidly around the base.

Now set q̇₁ = 0 and turn q̇₂ on. The green arrow appears, perpendicular to the second link this time — because joint 2 rotates only the second link around the elbow, so its contribution to the tip velocity is whatever vector that rotation produces at the wrist offset. That's column 2 of J scaled by q̇₂.

Finally, click Singular pose. The two arrows now point in the same direction — both red and green are along the same line. No combination of q̇₁ and q̇₂ can produce a velocity perpendicular to that line. The det(J) gauge has crashed to zero. You have just met your first singularity, geometrically and numerically at the same time.

Read the matrix and the arrows together

Column 1 of the displayed J should always equal (red arrow length × cos/sin of red arrow direction) divided by your current q̇₁ (in rad/s). Same for column 2 and the green arrow. The blue arrow is the column-wise weighted sum. When you see the picture and the numbers move together, ẋ = J · q̇ stops being a formula and starts being a sentence about geometry.

The 6×n geometric Jacobian

For a spatial robot with n joints, the end-effector has both linear and angular velocity — six components in total. The geometric Jacobian is therefore a 6×n matrix:

Numerical Jacobian — the cheap fallback

If you don't feel like deriving an analytical Jacobian for a 7-DoF arm by hand — and most weeks, I don't — you can compute J numerically with finite differences. Evaluate FK at q, perturb each joint by a small ε, evaluate FK at q + ε·eᵢ, take the difference and divide by ε. Each iteration gives you one column.

The sandbox below builds both the analytical and the numerical Jacobian for the 2-link arm at a specific configuration, and compares them. They should agree to about ε precision.

When to use which

Analytical: faster, exact, but you have to derive it once per robot. Numerical: slower (n+1 FK evaluations per call), accurate to about √ε, but generic — drop in any FK function and you get a Jacobian. In production controllers, analytical wins. In research code where you change robots twice a week, numerical wins.

Two ways to use J

The Jacobian is the door between joint space and task space, and you'll walk through that door in two directions. Both are coming up in the next two lectures.

Forward direction (this is easy): given joint velocities, compute end-effector velocity. Just the matrix-vector product ẋ = J · q̇. This is what the widget above is doing every time you move a slider.

Inverse direction (this is most of robotics): given a desired end-effector velocity, find joint velocities. Solve q̇ = J⁻¹ · ẋ — or, when J is non-square or singular, use a pseudo-inverse J⁺. Lecture 12 builds Newton–Raphson IK on top of this. Lecture 13 looks at what goes wrong near det(J) = 0 and how to damp the pseudo-inverse so the robot doesn't fly off the handle.

Quick recap

  • The Jacobian J(q) is the linear approximation of FK: ẋ = J(q) · q̇.
  • The i-th column of J is what the end-effector does when joint i alone moves.
  • For a 2-link planar arm, J is 2×2 and det(J) = L₁ L₂ sin(q₂). Singular at q₂ = 0 or ±π.
  • Geometric Jacobian for a spatial robot is 6×n, with the columns built from joint axes and origins of the DH chain.
  • Compute analytically when you have time to derive it, numerically (finite differences) when you don't.
  • Singularities are configurations where J loses rank — some directions of motion become unreachable.
