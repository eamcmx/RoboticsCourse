Lecture 17 · Module 4

Arduino + joysticks — building a teach-pendant

Industrial robots come with $5,000 teach-pendants. Yours costs €15 in joysticks. Map two analog sticks and a digital pin to six joint commands.

What the industrial pendant does

A teach-pendant is just a way to give the controller joint commands directly. The expensive ones have a touch screen and a dead-man switch and look like a piece of pro audio gear. Mechanically they're the same as two joysticks plus a button.

Industrial teach-pendants are physical interfaces with usually a single thumb-stick or trackball, a mode switch, and a dead-man button. The user picks a frame (base, tool, or joint) and a degree-of-freedom subset, then drives the robot incrementally. The mode switch toggles between joint subsets — typically (1, 2, 3) and (4, 5, 6).

The minimum hardware

Two analog joysticks (each gives two axes) plus one digital pin. Total: 4 analog + 1 digital. Wired to an Arduino:

  • Joystick 1: A0 (x), A1 (y) → q1, q2 rate command.
  • Joystick 2: A2 (x), A3 (y) → q3, q4 rate command.
  • Digital pin D2: mode toggle.

With mode = 0, axes drive joints 1–4. With mode = 1, axes drive joints 5–6 (and two are unused). With six DOFs and four sticks, you need either two modes or six sticks; one digital pin is the cheap solution.

The serial protocol

Arduino reads analog values, packs them as comma-separated text, sends over USB at 115200 baud:

Arduino → Host:    "512,512,512,512,0\n"
                    a0,  a1,  a2,  a3, mode

Host (MATLAB or Python) reads, converts to joint rate commands, integrates over time to maintain joint targets, and sends those targets to RoboDK or the real robot.

Quick recap

  • A teach-pendant is just a hardware front-end for joint commands.
  • Two joysticks plus a digital toggle covers six DOFs at a fraction of an industrial pendant's cost.
  • The host loop reads, integrates rates into targets, and forwards to the controller.

Wire and write the host loop

  1. Wire two joysticks and a button to an Arduino. Write the firmware that prints comma-separated values at 50 Hz.
  2. In MATLAB, write the host loop that reads the serial line, integrates rates into joint targets, and sends those targets to RoboDK's API.
  3. Drive the simulated UR5 from your real joystick. Demonstrate that the mode toggle correctly switches between joints (1–3) and (4–6).
